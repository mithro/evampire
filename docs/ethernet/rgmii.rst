RGMII interface
===============

This page describes the RGMII interface used between evampire and each
external Ethernet transceiver in the first version of the chip. It covers the signals, clocking, data rates,
timing and electrical levels, the separate management interface used to
configure the transceiver, and what all of this means for building the
interface in the gf180mcu process. The full design integrates the
transceiver on evampire; `The internal byte interface`_ describes which parts
of this page still apply then.

What RGMII is
-------------

An Ethernet port is split between two chips.

* The **Ethernet transceiver**, usually called the *PHY* (short for
  "physical layer device"), connects to the cable through the coupling
  transformers. It turns bytes into the analog signals on the cable and back
  again, recovers the clock of the incoming signal, and works out the speed
  of the link with the device at the other end of the cable.
* The **digital side**, which Ethernet documentation calls the *MAC* (short
  for "media access controller"), deals only in bytes. It sends and receives
  whole frames: it adds and checks the start pattern and the checksum, and
  decides when a frame may be sent. evampire is the digital side of its two
  ports.

*RGMII* (Reduced Gigabit Media Independent Interface) is the set of digital
signals between these two chips. "Media independent" means the digital side
does not need to know what kind of cable the transceiver drives. RGMII is not
an IEEE standard; it was published as a short industry specification
([RGMII13]_, [RGMII20]_) and is supported by many gigabit Ethernet
transceivers, including the ones referred to on this page.

How RGMII relates to MII and GMII
---------------------------------

RGMII is a pin-saving version of two older interfaces defined in the IEEE
802.3 Ethernet standard [IEEE8023]_:

MII (Media Independent Interface)
   Used for 10 and 100 Mbit/s. It moves 4 bits per clock cycle on one edge
   of a 2.5 MHz or 25 MHz clock ([RGMII20]_ section 5.0).

GMII (Gigabit Media Independent Interface)
   Used for 1000 Mbit/s. It moves 8 bits per clock cycle on the rising edge
   of a 125 MHz clock, together with a "data valid" and an "error" signal in
   each direction ([RGMII20]_ section 3.4, [DP83867]_ sections 6.10 and
   6.11).

RGMII carries exactly the same information as GMII, but uses both edges of
the clock so that only 4 data wires are needed in each direction, and folds
the "valid" and "error" signals into one control wire per direction. This
reduces the interface from 24 to 12 signals ([DP83867]_ section 7.4.1.1).
The names of the GMII signals (for example "receive data valid", RX_DV, and
"receive error", RX_ER) are still used to describe what the RGMII control
wire means.

Signals
-------

.. list-table:: RGMII signals. "Out" and "In" are seen from evampire.
   :header-rows: 1
   :widths: 14 10 76

   * - Signal
     - Direction
     - Meaning
   * - TXC
     - Out
     - Transmit clock. Generated by evampire: 125 MHz at 1000 Mbit/s,
       25 MHz at 100 Mbit/s, 2.5 MHz at 10 Mbit/s.
   * - TX_CTL
     - Out
     - Transmit control. "Transmit enable" on the rising edge of TXC and
       a combination of "transmit enable" and "transmit error" on the falling
       edge (see `Control signal encoding`_).
   * - TXD[3:0]
     - Out
     - Transmit data. Bits 3:0 of each byte on the rising edge of TXC and
       bits 7:4 on the falling edge.
   * - RXC
     - In
     - Receive clock. Generated by the transceiver from the signal arriving
       on the cable, at the same three frequencies. It runs continuously,
       also between frames.
   * - RX_CTL
     - In
     - Receive control. "Receive data valid" on the rising edge of RXC and
       a combination of "data valid" and "receive error" on the falling edge.
   * - RXD[3:0]
     - In
     - Receive data. Bits 3:0 of each byte on the rising edge of RXC and bits
       7:4 on the falling edge. Between frames it can carry the link status
       (see `Between frames`_).

These definitions come from Table 1 of the RGMII specification
([RGMII13]_, [RGMII20]_). Transceiver datasheets often use slightly different
names for the same pins, for example GTX_CLK or TX_CLK for TXC, RX_CLK for
RXC, TX_EN or TX_CTRL for TX_CTL, RX_DV or RX_CTRL for RX_CTL, and TD/RD for
TXD/RXD.

Each port also uses the management interface (MDC and MDIO, described in
`Management interface (MDC and MDIO)`_). Unlike the data signals, the
management interface can be shared by several transceivers.

A link runs in *full duplex*, where both ends may send at the same time,
or in *half duplex*, where only one end may send at a time. Half duplex
needs two extra indications: "carrier sense" (the other end is sending) and
"collision" (both ends sent at once). RGMII has no separate wires for these;
they are recovered from RX_CTL and RXD ([RGMII20]_ section 3.4.2). evampire
uses full duplex only (see `Speed and duplex of the two ports`_), so it does
not need them.

1000 Mbit/s
~~~~~~~~~~~

At 1000 Mbit/s each clock cycle carries one byte: the low nibble (bits 3:0)
around the rising edge and the high nibble (bits 7:4) around the falling edge
([RGMII20]_ section 3.2). The two directions work the same way: the
transmitting chip sends its clock together with its data, and the receiving
chip uses that clock to sample the data. This is called *source-synchronous*
timing.

             then high nibble.

         "data": ["0x01", "0x23", "0x45"] }

:numref:`fig-rgmii-1000-byte` shows the order. A byte with the value 0x23
is sent as the nibble 3 on the rising edge followed by the nibble 2 on the
falling edge. Ethernet sends the least significant bit first on the cable,
and on the 4-bit MII data bit 0 is the bit that goes out first
([LAN91C111]_ section 7.5.4). RGMII keeps the same order: lower bits first.

10 and 100 Mbit/s
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

At 10 and 100 Mbit/s the clock slows to 2.5 MHz or 25 MHz and each clock
cycle carries one nibble instead of one byte. The specification says the
interface works "exactly the same way" as at 1000 Mbit/s, except that the
data may be repeated on the falling edge ([RGMII20]_ section 5.0). In
practice the data signals are sampled on the rising edge only, while
RX_CTL and TX_CTL are still sampled on both edges ([KSZ9031]_ section 7.1).
A byte takes two clock cycles: low nibble first, then high nibble.

             at 100 Mbit/s and 400 ns at 10 Mbit/s. Drawn with each nibble
             centred on the rising edge of RXC.

       { "name": "RX_CTL",   "wave": "1..........." },

The transceiver generates RXC at every speed. evampire generates TXC at
every speed. The clocks may have one stretched high or low pulse when the
transceiver changes speed, or when RXC switches from its free-running clock
to the clock recovered from a newly arriving frame, but they must not glitch
([RGMII20]_ section 5.0 and Table 2 note 3). Before evampire starts a frame
at a new speed, it must already be sending TXC at that speed: the
specification requires the digital side to hold TX_CTL low until it is
running at the same speed as the transceiver ([RGMII20]_ section 5.0).

Control signal encoding
-----------------------

The control wire carries two GMII signals per clock cycle
([RGMII20]_ section 3.4):

* on the **rising edge**: "data valid" (receive) or "transmit enable"
  (transmit);
* on the **falling edge**: "data valid" XOR "error".

The XOR is used so that the control wire does not toggle during normal
operation. It stays high for both edges for every good byte of a frame and
low for both edges between frames, which saves power. It only changes in the
middle of a clock cycle when something unusual happens, such as an error.

   * - 0, 0

   * - 1, 1
     - 1
     - 0
     - Normal byte of a frame.
   * - 1, 0
     - 1
     - 1
     - Byte of a frame that has an error. On receive: the transceiver
       detected a problem while receiving this byte. On transmit: tells the

   * - 0, 1
     - 0

     - Not a frame byte. The value on RXD/TXD gives the meaning, for example

In :numref:`fig-rgmii-ctl-encoding`, RX_CTL is high on both edges for each
good byte, and high-then-low for the byte with the error. The receiving
logic recovers the two GMII signals as

.. math::

   \text{valid} = \text{CTL}_\text{rise}, \qquad
   \text{error} = \text{CTL}_\text{rise} \oplus \text{CTL}_\text{fall}.

Between frames
--------------

When RX_CTL is low on both edges, no frame is being received. The
specification defines an optional *in-band status*: during these gaps the
transceiver puts its link state on RXD[3:0], repeated on both edges
([RGMII20]_ section 3.4.1 and Table 4).

.. list-table:: In-band status on RXD[3:0] while RX_CTL is 0, 0
   :header-rows: 1
   :widths: 15 25 60

   * - Bit
     - Meaning
     - Values
   * - RXD[0]
     - Link status
     - 0 = link down, 1 = link up
   * - RXD[2:1]
     - Speed, given as the RXC frequency
     - 00 = 2.5 MHz (10 Mbit/s), 01 = 25 MHz (100 Mbit/s),
       10 = 125 MHz (1000 Mbit/s), 11 = reserved
   * - RXD[3]
     - Duplex
     - 0 = half duplex, 1 = full duplex

For example, a gigabit full-duplex link that is up shows the nibble 1101
(0xD) between frames. While the link is down, the reported speed and duplex
are the transceiver's own settings ([RGMII20]_ section 3.4.1).

Because this status is optional, not every transceiver sends it. The
Microchip KSZ9031RNX always sends it ([KSZ9031]_ section 3.9.4). evampire
therefore reads the link state through the management interface, which every
transceiver supports, and can use in-band status as an extra, faster
indication where it is available.

Frame structure on the interface
--------------------------------

A *frame* is one Ethernet message. On RXD and TXD it is sent as a sequence
of bytes, each byte as two nibbles, low nibble first.

.. list-table:: Fields of a frame in the order they are sent
                ([ENC28J60]_ section 5.1; preamble and SFD bit patterns from
                [LAN91C111]_ section 7.5.4)
   :header-rows: 1
   :widths: 24 14 62

   * - Field
     - Bytes
     - Contents
   * - Preamble
     - 7
     - The byte 0x55 (bit pattern 10101010 in the order sent) repeated.
       It gives the receiver time to lock on to the signal.
   * - Start-of-frame delimiter (SFD)
     - 1
     - The byte 0xD5 (bits 10101011 in the order sent). It marks the end of
       the preamble: the next byte is the first byte of the frame.
   * - Destination MAC address
     - 6
     - The address of the device the frame is for.
   * - Source MAC address
     - 6
     - The address of the device that sent the frame.
   * - Type or length
     - 2
     - The protocol carried in the payload (or, for values up to 1500, the
       payload length).
   * - Payload
     - 46 to 1500
     - The data. Shorter data is padded to 46 bytes.
   * - Frame check sequence (FCS)
     - 4
     - A 32-bit checksum (CRC) over the addresses, type, payload and
       padding.

A *MAC address* is the 48-bit address that identifies a device on an
Ethernet network. From the destination address to the checksum, a normal
frame is 64 to 1518 bytes long ([ENC28J60]_ section 5.1). An optional 4-byte
tag that separates virtual networks on the same cable increases the maximum
to 1522 bytes; larger "jumbo" frames are also used on some networks (the
KSZ9031RNX supports frames up to 16 KB, [KSZ9031]_ features list).

On the cable the preamble and SFD together are 62 bits of alternating
1,0,1,0,... followed by 1,1 ([LAN91C111]_ section 7.5.4). Because the bits
are sent least significant first, the last eight of these bits,
1,0,1,0,1,0,1,1, form the byte 1101 0101 = 0xD5, and each earlier group of
eight forms the byte 0x55. On a 4-bit interface this is the nibble 5
repeated, followed by the nibbles 5 and D; the LAN91C111 looks for exactly
this "5Dh" pattern to find the start of a frame ([LAN91C111]_
section 7.5.4). On RGMII the preamble is therefore the nibble 5 repeated
fourteen times, and the SFD is the nibble 5 followed by the nibble D.

Two details matter for a design that forwards frames:

* **The preamble may arrive shorter than 7 bytes.** A transceiver is not
  required to pass the whole preamble through. For example, the KSZ9031RNX
  removes all 7 preamble bytes when receiving at 10 Mbit/s and starts the
  frame on RXD with the SFD ([KSZ9031]_ section 3.1.5), and the 10 Mbit/s
  receiver of the LAN91C111 loses part of the preamble while it locks on,
  passing at least 6 nibbles of it ([LAN91C111]_ section 7.7.4.3). The
  receiving logic
  must therefore look for the SFD byte instead of counting preamble bytes,
  and the transmit side must generate a full preamble itself.
* **There is a minimum gap between frames.** A transmitter must leave a
  minimum gap between the end of one frame and the start of the next
  preamble. At 10 Mbit/s this is "the minimum IEEE specified Inter-Packet
  Gap (IPG) of 9.6 µs" ([ENC28J60]_ register MABBIPG), which is 96 bit
  times. IEEE 802.3 gives the gap as 96 bit times at every speed
  ([IEEE8023]_ clause 4.4.2), which is 12 byte times or 96 ns at
  1000 Mbit/s.

Timing
------

Why the clock must be delayed
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

The transmitting chip launches its clock and its data at the same moment:
the data changes at every clock edge ([RGMII20]_ section 3.3). If the
receiving chip sampled the data with that clock unchanged, it would sample
exactly while the data is changing. The clock therefore has to be delayed,
somewhere between the two chips' flip-flops, so that each clock edge arrives
in the middle of the data it samples.

At 1000 Mbit/s the data changes every half clock period, so the middle of
the data is a quarter period after the edge:

.. math::

   t_\text{delay} = \frac{T_\text{cyc}}{4} = \frac{8\ \text{ns}}{4}
   = 2\ \text{ns}.

That is where the "2 ns" in every RGMII document comes from.

Who adds the delay
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

The two versions of the specification differ in where the delay is added.

Version 1.3 (2000-12-10): delay added by the circuit board
   Each chip outputs clock and data together, within ±500 ps of each other
   (TskewT). The receiving chip needs the clock to arrive 1.0 to 2.6 ns
   after the data (TskewR). The specification states that this "implies"
   that the circuit board adds 1.5 to 2.0 ns of extra trace delay to each
   clock signal ([RGMII13]_ Table 2 and note 1).

Version 2.0 (2002-04-01): delay added inside a chip
   A chip may instead delay the clock internally. A transmitter with an
   internal delay guarantees at least 1.2 ns of setup and 1.2 ns of hold
   time at its own pins (TsetupT, TholdT), and the receiver must accept as
   little as 1.0 ns of setup and 1.0 ns of hold (TsetupR, TholdR). A device
   with an internal delay is called "RGMII-ID" ([RGMII20]_ Table 2, note 4).
   A device may offer both modes and still meet the specification.

Timing is measured at half the I/O supply voltage ([RGMII20]_ section 3.3).
The skew numbers are defined only for 1000 Mbit/s. At 10 and 100 Mbit/s the
clock period is 5 or 50 times longer, so the margins are much larger; the
KSZ9031RNX datasheet relaxes the maximum TskewR to 16 ns at 100 Mbit/s and
160 ns at 10 Mbit/s ([KSZ9031]_ section 7.1).

For each direction, the delay must be added **exactly once**: by the board,
by the transmitting chip or by the receiving chip. Adding it twice moves the
clock edge to the next data transition and fails just as badly as not
adding it at all.

.. list-table:: Where the 2 ns delay can be added for each direction
   :header-rows: 1
   :widths: 22 39 39

   * - Added by
     - Transmit (evampire to transceiver)
     - Receive (transceiver to evampire)
   * - Circuit board
     - TXC trace longer than TXD by about 2 ns
     - RXC trace longer than RXD by about 2 ns
   * - Transmitting chip
     - evampire delays TXC
     - Transceiver delays RXC
   * - Receiving chip
     - Transceiver delays TXC internally
     - evampire delays RXC internally

At the propagation delay quoted in the DP83867 layout guidelines (11 ps per
60 mil, about 7.2 ps/mm, on standard FR-4 board material; [DP83867]_
section 9.2.2.1.1), a 2 ns board delay needs about

.. math::

   \frac{2000\ \text{ps}}{7.2\ \text{ps/mm}} \approx 280\ \text{mm}

of extra clock trace, folded into a meander. The same guidelines recommend
keeping RGMII traces shorter than 2 inches (51 mm), with 6 inches (152 mm) as
the maximum.

     - 1.2 ns typical delay by default, adjustable up to 2.58 ns with the
       pad-skew registers ([KSZ9031]_ section 3.9.3).

TI's application note SNLA243 gives a worked timing budget for the
transmit path into a DP83867 with its delay set to 2.0 ns. It subtracts
0.2 ns of delay variation, 0.5 ns of transmitter skew (TskewT), 0.35 ns of
I/O buffer skew, 0.1 ns of board skew and the DP83867's own input
requirement (0.5 ns setup, 0.25 ns hold), and uses a 3.6 ns clock high time
(45 % duty cycle) for the hold side. That leaves 0.35 ns of setup margin and
0.2 ns of hold margin ([SNLA243]_ sections 3.2 and 3.3). The margins are
small even with a well-behaved transmitter.

Clock frequency and duty cycle
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

* **Frequency.** The RGMII specification gives TXC and RXC as 125, 25 or
  2.5 MHz "±50 ppm" ([RGMII20]_ Table 1). The 125 MHz GMII transmit clock
  of the DP83867 is specified at ±100 ppm ([DP83867]_ section 6.10,
  parameter T5). RXC follows the crystal of the device at the far end of the
  cable, so evampire is designed for a receive clock that differs from its
  own transmit clock by up to 200 ppm (two crystals each up to 100 ppm away
  from nominal).
* **Period.** Each clock cycle may be 7.2 to 8.8 ns long at 1000 Mbit/s
  (Tcyc in the table above). This is a limit on individual cycles, not on
  the average frequency.
* **Duty cycle.** 45 % to 55 % at 1000 Mbit/s, so each half of the clock is
  at least 3.6 ns long. 40 % to 60 % at 10 and 100 Mbit/s. The duty cycle
  may be stretched briefly during speed changes ([RGMII20]_ Table 2,
  note 3).
* **Edges.** Rise and fall times (20 % to 80 %) of at most 0.75 ns.

Board design
~~~~~~~~~~~~

* **Trace impedance and length.** The DP83867 layout guidelines ask for
  50 Ω traces, kept shorter than 2 inches (51 mm) where possible and never
  longer than 6 inches (152 mm) ([DP83867]_ section 9.2.2.1.1).
* **Length matching.** All transmit traces must match each other in length,
  and so must all receive traces. The DP83867 guidelines limit the skew
  within TXD[3:0] and within RXD[3:0] to 11 ps, about 60 mil (1.5 mm) of
  trace ([DP83867]_ sections 9.2.2.1.1 and 9.4.1).
* **Source termination.** A trace a few centimetres long is long compared
  with sub-nanosecond edges, so it behaves as a transmission line.
  Reflections are controlled by making the driver's
  output resistance plus any series resistor equal to the trace impedance,
  with the resistor placed next to the driving pin. The DP83867 trims its
  own RGMII output impedance to about 50 Ω (adjustable from about 35 to
  70 Ω) so that it needs no external resistor ([DP83867]_ register 0x0170,
  IO_IMPEDANCE_CTRL). evampire's outputs (TXC, TX_CTL, TXD) need a series
  resistor whose value depends on the output resistance of the gf180mcu pad,
  which is not given in the I/O library documentation and must be found by
  simulation or measurement.
* **Avoid vias and stubs** on the RGMII traces ([DP83867]_ section 9.4.1).

Management interface (MDC and MDIO)
-----------------------------------

Each transceiver has a few dozen 16-bit registers that report its status
(is a cable connected, at what speed, in which duplex mode) and control its
settings (reset, speed choice, clock delays). They are read and written over
a two-wire serial interface defined in Clause 22 of IEEE 802.3 [IEEE8023]_:

MDC
   Management clock, always driven by evampire. Data is sampled on its
   rising edge ([DP83867]_ section 7.4.2). It does not need to run
   continuously.

MDIO
   Management data, bidirectional. Driven by evampire, except during the
   data part of a read, when the transceiver drives it. A pull-up resistor
   keeps it high when nobody drives it: 2.2 kΩ for the DP83867
   ([DP83867]_ section 7.4.2), 1.0 kΩ to 4.7 kΩ for the KSZ9031RNX
   ([KSZ9031]_ pin description of MDIO).

Several transceivers can share one MDC/MDIO pair. Each transceiver has an
address set by pins sampled at power-up, and responds only to frames carrying
its address. evampire can therefore drive both of its transceivers from one
pair of pins, provided the two are set to different addresses by their
configuration pins.

Clock rate
   Clause 22 sets a minimum MDC period of 400 ns, that is at most 2.5 MHz.
   The Linux MDIO driver uses this limit ([LINUX-MDIO]_ comment on
   MDIO_DELAY), and the KSZ9031RNX datasheet gives 2.5 MHz as the typical
   rate ([KSZ9031]_ Table 7-3). Many transceivers accept more (the DP83867
   up to 25 MHz, [DP83867]_ section 6.8), but evampire uses 2.5 MHz or less
   so that any transceiver works. One register access takes 64 MDC cycles,
   about 26 µs at 2.5 MHz, which is far faster than the link status changes.

Frame format
   Every access is one 64-bit frame, sent most significant bit first within
   each field ([KSZ9031]_ Table 3-8; bit order as in [LINUX-MDIO]_):

Registers evampire uses
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

*Autonegotiation* is the exchange, at link start-up, in which the two
transceivers on a cable tell each other which speeds and duplex modes they
support and both pick the best common one. *1000BASE-T* is the IEEE 802.3
name for 1000 Mbit/s over the usual four-pair copper cable.

Registers 0 to 15 are defined by IEEE 802.3 and have the same meaning in
every transceiver; registers 16 to 31 are specific to each manufacturer
([KSZ9031]_ section 4.2). The bit definitions below are taken from the
DP83867 datasheet ([DP83867]_ sections 8.1 to 8.13) and agree with the
KSZ9031RNX datasheet ([KSZ9031]_ section 4.2).

.. list-table:: Standard transceiver registers used by evampire
   :header-rows: 1
   :widths: 10 22 68

   * - Register
     - Name
     - Bits used
   * - 0
     - Basic control
     - Bit 15: reset (write 1; reads 1 until the reset is complete).
       Bit 12: autonegotiation enable. Bit 9: restart autonegotiation
       (clears itself). When autonegotiation is off, bits 6 and 13 force the
       speed (10 = 1000, 01 = 100, 00 = 10 Mbit/s) and bit 8 the duplex.
   * - 1
     - Basic status
     - Bit 2: link status, 1 = link up. This bit stays 0 after a link
       failure until it is read, so it is read twice to get the present
       state. Bit 5: autonegotiation complete.
   * - 2, 3
     - Identifier
     - Manufacturer and model number, for checking which transceiver is
       fitted.
   * - 4
     - Autonegotiation advertisement
     - Speeds this end offers: bit 8 = 100 Mbit/s full duplex, bit 7 =
       100 Mbit/s half duplex, bit 6 = 10 Mbit/s full duplex, bit 5 =
       10 Mbit/s half duplex. Bits 4:0 = 00001 (IEEE 802.3).
   * - 5
     - Link partner ability
     - The same bits as register 4, as offered by the far end.
   * - 9
     - 1000BASE-T control
     - Bit 9: offer 1000 Mbit/s full duplex. Bit 8: offer 1000 Mbit/s half
       duplex.
   * - 10
     - 1000BASE-T status
     - Bit 11: far end offers 1000 Mbit/s full duplex. Bit 10: far end
       offers 1000 Mbit/s half duplex.

The result of autonegotiation is the highest speed
offered by both ends (in registers 4 and 9 at this end, 5 and 10 at the far
end). Most transceivers also report the result directly in a
manufacturer-specific register, for example register 0x11 of the DP83867
(bits 15:14 speed, bit 13 duplex, bit 11 "speed and duplex resolved",
bit 10 link up; [DP83867]_ section 8.15).

Registers above 15, and most of the extended registers reached indirectly
through registers 13 and 14 (0x0D and 0x0E), are specific to each
transceiver model ([DP83867]_ section 8.12, [KSZ9031]_ section 3.9.3). The clock
delay settings described in `Delay options in real transceivers`_ are in
this space, so the processor's start-up code depends on which transceiver is
fitted. It identifies the transceiver from registers 2 and 3.

I/O voltage
~~~~~~~~~~~

evampire runs its RGMII and MDIO pins at **3.3 V**.

* The wafer.space gf180mcu project template offers its I/O pad libraries,
  including the foundry library gf180mcu_fd_io, at 5 V or 3.3 V
  ([WAFERSPACE]_). With gf180mcu_fd_io the I/O supply and the core supply
  are connected together, so the core logic also runs at 3.3 V.
* The transceivers' RGMII pins run at 3.3 V at most. 5 V signalling is
  outside their ratings: with its 3.3 V I/O option the DP83867 allows at
  most 3.8 V on its RGMII pins ([DP83867]_ section 6.1), and the KSZ9031RNX
  allows at most 5.0 V on any input ([KSZ9031]_ section 5.1), below the
  5.5 V upper limit of a 5 V supply.
* The gf180mcu_fd_io library is also characterised with a 2.5 V supply
  (liberty corners 2v25, 2v50 and 2v75 for gf180mcu_fd_io__bi_t,
  [GF180IO]_), but the wafer.space template does not list 2.5 V as an
  option, and the gf180mcu_fd_sc_mcu9t5v0 standard cells are characterised
  only around 1.8 V, 3.3 V and 5 V ([GF180SC]_). Because this pad library
  ties the core supply to the I/O supply, 2.5 V is not used.

The gf180mcu_fd_io datasheet gives input thresholds of 0.8 V (low) and
2.0 V (high) and output currents for a 4.5 V to 5.5 V supply only
([GF180IO]_ "Electrical Characteristics"). The thresholds at 3.3 V are
**to be checked** against the 3.3 V output levels of the chosen transceiver
(for example output high at least 2.0 V and output low at most 0.6 V for the
DP83867).

What this means:

* **Absolute delays do not matter much.** TXC goes through the same type of
  pad as TXD, and RXC through the same type of pad as RXD, so the pad delay
  is common to clock and data and cancels.
* **Rising and falling delays differ** by 0.1 to 0.3 ns. On TXC this
  changes the duty cycle: at the slow corner the high time grows by about
  0.27 ns, from 4.00 ns to about 4.27 ns, or 53 %. This is inside the 45 %
  to 55 % limit but uses up much of it, before adding any imbalance in
  evampire's internal 125 MHz clock.
* **The output edges are slower than RGMII asks for.** RGMII allows at most
  0.75 ns from 20 % to 80 %. The liberty edge times above are 0.53 to
  1.89 ns; the measurement thresholds used for them are not stated in the
  per-cell liberty files and are **to be checked**. At the slow corner the
  edges take a large part of the 4 ns half period. 10 and 100 Mbit/s are not
  affected. For 1000 Mbit/s the signal at the transceiver input must be
  checked with a board-level simulation of the pad, trace and transceiver
  input.

Transmit path: generating both-edge data
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

The gf180mcu standard-cell libraries have flip-flops that load on the rising
edge (dffq) and on the falling edge (dffnq), and a two-input selector
(mux2) ([GF180SC]_). Each RGMII output is built from these, as shown in
:numref:`fig-rgmii-tx-ddr`.

.. _fig-rgmii-tx-ddr:

.. graphviz:: rgmii/tx-ddr.dot
   :caption: Output stage for one TXD bit. The selector passes flip-flop A
             while the transmit clock is high and flip-flop B while it is
             low. Each flip-flop changes only while the selector is passing
             the other one, so the output cannot glitch.
   :align: center

* The byte to send is held in a register that loads on the rising edge of
  the 125 MHz transmit clock.
* Flip-flop A loads the low nibble on the falling edge. It is then stable
  for the whole following high phase, when the selector passes it to the pad.
* Flip-flop B loads the high nibble on the rising edge. It is then stable
  for the whole following low phase, when the selector passes it to the pad.
* The pad output therefore changes only when the clock changes, a fixed
  selector-and-pad delay after each clock edge.

**TXC is generated by the same structure**, with flip-flop A fed a constant
1 and flip-flop B a constant 0. TXC then has the same delay from the internal
clock as every TXD bit, which keeps the clock-to-data skew at the pins within
the ±500 ps of TskewT. The clock and data selectors and pads are placed next
to each other and routed alike. The paths from the byte register to
flip-flop A are half-cycle paths (4 ns) and must be kept short; at the slow
corner (SS, 125 °C, 3.0 V) a gf180mcu_fd_sc_mcu9t5v0 dffq_1 flip-flop alone
has a clock-to-output delay of about 1.4 to 1.5 ns with a light load
([GF180SC]_ liberty tables).

An alternative output stage uses one rising-edge and one falling-edge
flip-flop whose outputs are combined by an XOR gate. Either works; the
selector form is described here because its output delay is the same for
every bit.

Receive path: capturing both-edge data
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

.. _fig-rgmii-rx-ddr:

.. graphviz:: rgmii/rx-ddr.dot
   :caption: Input stage for one RXD bit. Flip-flop R captures the low nibble
             on the rising edge of RXC and flip-flop F captures the high
             nibble on the falling edge. On the next rising edge both are
             copied into the byte register, which feeds the rate-matching
             buffer.
   :align: center

As shown in :numref:`fig-rgmii-rx-ddr`:

* Flip-flop R loads RXD[n] on the rising edge of RXC: bit n of the byte.
* Flip-flop F loads RXD[n] on the falling edge: bit n + 4.
* On the next rising edge the byte register loads both. Flip-flop R is
  updated by the same edge, but the byte register takes the value from just
  before the edge, so it holds the complete byte. The path from F to the
  byte register is a half-cycle (4 ns) path.
* RX_CTL is captured the same way. The valid and error signals follow from
  the equation in `Control signal encoding`_.

The capture flip-flops sit next to their input pads and are clocked by RXC
through a small local clock buffer, not by the chip's main clock tree. The
receive window at the pins is narrow: with the clock delayed inside the
transceiver, evampire must accept data that is valid only 1.0 ns before and
1.0 ns after each RXC edge (TsetupR, TholdR). Inside the chip, any delay on
the clock path that the data path does not have moves the sampling point
later and eats into the 1.0 ns hold time. With a slow-corner buffer delay of
about 0.36 ns per stage (gf180mcu_fd_sc_mcu9t5v0 buf_2, [GF180SC]_), two
stages of clock buffering would already use most of it. The clock buffer is
therefore balanced by an equal delay in the data paths, or compensated by
setting a slightly shorter delay in the transceiver (see the next section).
The resulting setup and hold margins at all corners are **to be checked**
with static timing analysis, using RXC as a clock that enters at the RXC pin
and RXD and RX_CTL as inputs that are valid from 1.0 ns before to 1.0 ns
after each RXC edge.

Where the 2 ns delay comes from
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

**evampire adds no delay itself. The transceiver adds the 2 ns delay in both
directions:** on TXC inside the transceiver before it samples TXD, and on
RXC before it leaves the transceiver. evampire outputs TXC edge-aligned with
TXD (meeting TskewT) and receives RXC already centred in RXD (TsetupR,
TholdR).

The reasons are:

* **gf180mcu has no accurate on-chip delay.** The delay of a standard cell
  changes by about a factor of three between the fast and slow corners
  (gf180mcu_fd_sc_mcu9t5v0 buf_2 with a light load: about 0.12 ns at FF,
  0.19 ns at TT and 0.36 ns at SS, [GF180SC]_). A chain of cells that gives
  2 ns at the typical corner would give about 1.3 ns at the fast corner and
  3.8 ns at the slow corner, outside the 1.0 to 2.6 ns window of TskewR.
  A delay that tracks the clock period would need a delay-locked loop or a
  clock at a multiple of 125 MHz, which adds design risk for no benefit.
* **Transceivers already have trimmed, adjustable delays.** The DP83867
  adds 0.25 to 4.00 ns in 0.25 ns steps in each direction. The delay can
  be tuned after the chips are built, through MDIO, to centre the sampling
  point and to compensate for evampire's own clock buffering.
* **Board delay is impractical.** About 280 mm of extra trace per clock
  (see `Who adds the delay`_) is longer than the 152 mm maximum trace length
  the DP83867 guidelines recommend.

This choice limits which transceivers can be used without board changes.
The DP83867 supports it directly. The KSZ9031RNX can add only up to 1.38 ns
on its transmit clock input ([KSZ9031]_ section 3.9.3), so with that part
the board must add the rest of the transmit delay (about 0.6 ns or more,
roughly 85 mm of extra TXC trace at 7.2 ps/mm). The processor's start-up code
sets the delay registers of the fitted transceiver over MDIO, so the
power-up pin settings of the transceiver do not have to be correct.

10 and 100 Mbit/s inside evampire
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

The internal logic always runs from the 125 MHz clock. At lower speeds it
uses a clock enable instead of a slower clock:

* **Transmit.** At 100 Mbit/s one nibble is sent every 5 cycles of
  125 MHz, at 10 Mbit/s every 50 cycles. TXC is generated by the output
  stage of :numref:`fig-rgmii-tx-ddr` with a pattern that stays high for
  5 half cycles and low for 5 half cycles (100 Mbit/s), or 50 and 50
  (10 Mbit/s). This gives 25 MHz or 2.5 MHz with a 50 % duty cycle,
  inside the 40 % to 60 % limit. TXD changes only at the start of each TXC
  cycle and is the same on both edges. TX_CTL keeps its two-edge meaning.
* **Receive.** The same capture flip-flops are used. At these speeds the
  byte is assembled from the rising-edge samples of two consecutive RXC
  cycles, low nibble first. Because the preamble may arrive with an odd
  number of nibbles, the byte boundary is found from the SFD: the first
  nibble D after a run of nibble 5 is the high nibble of the SFD, and the
  next nibble is the low nibble of the first byte of the frame. RX_CTL is
  still decoded from both edges.

The internal byte interface
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Behind the both-edge input and output stages, each RGMII port presents a
simple interface to the rest of evampire: one byte per clock cycle in each
direction, with a "data valid" flag and an "error" flag, plus a clock enable
at 10 and 100 Mbit/s. On the receive side these come from the byte register
of :numref:`fig-rgmii-rx-ddr` and the decoded RX_CTL; on the transmit side
they feed the byte register of :numref:`fig-rgmii-tx-ddr` and TX_CTL. This
is the same information as the GMII signals described in
`How RGMII relates to MII and GMII`_.

This interface is the boundary between the port and the forwarding logic.
An integrated transceiver in the full design connects to the forwarding
logic through the same interface, so the rate-matching buffers, output
selectors, address filter and frame buffers are the same in both cases.

Clock domains
~~~~~~~~~~~~~

.. _fig-rgmii-clock-domains:

             them.
   :align: center

* **RXC of port A** and **RXC of port B**: each is recovered by its

  the read side of both rate-matching buffers, the address filter and the
  frame buffers.
* **The processor clock**, which also drives the MDC/MDIO controller.

The crossings are:

* **Crossings 1 and 2, port A or B receive clock to 125 MHz**: through the
  rate-matching buffers described in :doc:`../architecture/index`. These
  are small dual-clock buffers whose read and write positions cross between
  the clocks in Gray code (a count in which only one bit changes per step,
  so that a value sampled while it changes is off by at most one). The write
  clock may stop, slow down to 2.5 MHz, or have stretched pulses when the
  link changes, so the buffer is emptied and restarted when a link goes
  down.
* **Crossing 3, 125 MHz to the processor clock**, if the two differ: through
  the frame buffers. Link status taken from in-band status changes slowly
  and crosses through a two-flip-flop synchroniser.

At 1000 Mbit/s the receive clock may be up to 200 ppm faster or slower than
the transmit clock. Over one frame of length :math:`L` bytes the buffer
fills or drains by

.. math::

   \Delta = L \times 200 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{bytes},

which is 0.3 bytes for a 1522-byte frame and 1.8 bytes for a 9000-byte
frame. The buffer must hold this drift plus its synchronisation delay and
the difference between the received and the regenerated preamble (up to
7 bytes, see `Frame structure on the interface`_).

Speed and duplex of the two ports
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Cut-through forwarding sends each byte out of the other port a few clock
cycles after it arrives. This only works if both ports run at the same
speed: a frame arriving at 1000 Mbit/s cannot be sent at 100 Mbit/s without
storing almost all of it, and a frame arriving at 100 Mbit/s cannot be sent
at 1000 Mbit/s because the transmitter would run out of data after the first
byte. evampire does not have the memory to store whole forwarded frames.

evampire therefore runs both ports at the same speed, in full duplex:

* The processor configures both transceivers to advertise only full-duplex
  speeds (registers 4 and 9). Half duplex is not offered, because it needs
  the digital side to detect collisions and resend frames, which a
  cut-through path without frame storage cannot do.
* When both links are up, the processor reads the resolved speed of each
  port. If the speeds are equal, it turns on forwarding between the ports.
* If the speeds differ, forwarding stays off and frames arriving on either
  port are not forwarded. The processor removes the higher speeds from the
  advertisement of the faster port and restarts autonegotiation on that
  port, so that it comes up again at the speed of the slower port. Frames
  to and from the processor are still received and sent on each port while
  forwarding is off.
* When a link goes down, forwarding is turned off and that port's
  transceiver advertises every full-duplex speed again.

.. _fig-rgmii-speed-policy:

.. graphviz:: rgmii/speed-policy.dot
   :caption: How the processor keeps the two ports at the same speed.
   :align: center

:numref:`fig-rgmii-speed-policy` summarises this. A forwarded frame that
arrives with RX_CTL showing an error is passed on with TX_CTL showing an
error on the same byte (TX_CTL = 1, 0), so the far end discards it; because
the frame is already being sent, it cannot be dropped instead.
